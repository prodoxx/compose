### 0w. Write tickets a person can read at a glance
This applies to every ticket compose writes: epics, features, stories, tasks, design stories and tasks, and the GitHub issues the toolsmith files.
The reader is a person who was not in the conversation: the product owner, a reviewer, or a new engineer.
They should understand the ticket on the first read.
Write the way a good engineer writes a ticket for a teammate: direct, concrete, and plain.

**Title**
- Say what changes and where, in everyday words.
- Lead with the area (a screen, component, script, or command), then the change: `Score ring: show 20 colours and dim unlit blocks`.
- One change per title.
  If it needs "and" to join two changes, it is probably two tickets.
- Keep it under 80 characters; `compose linear create` and `update --title` refuse longer ones.
- Leave out exact values, internal names, and shorthand the reader has to decode (`18/26`, `Head padding`, `chain base`).
  Put them in the description.
- A bug title says what is broken, as the user sees it: `Job card: match label is unreadable on dark backgrounds`.

**Description**
- Open with one to three plain sentences: what is wrong or missing today, what changes, and why it matters.
  Someone who reads only this opening knows what the ticket is about.
- A feature and a story open with one user story sentence in place of that opening: `As a <role or persona>, I want <goal or feature>, so that <reason, benefit or value>.`
  On a story it is the first line of the description; on a feature it is the first line of `## Intent`.
  Name a real role from the product (`As a shopper`, `As a store admin`), never `As a user` when a closer one exists, and never the system or a developer.
  The reason is what the person gains, in their terms, never a restatement of the goal.
  Example: `As a shopper, I want my cart kept when I sign in, so that I do not have to find everything again.`
  One or two plain sentences of context may follow when the reader needs them.
- Then give the details the builder needs: exact names, values, files, and steps.
  Use lists and code formatting for them, and keep them out of the opening.
  On an epic, feature or story they go in the planning and decision log (below), never in the part people read.
- Use short sentences, active voice, and one fact per sentence.
- Name things the way the product owner and the code name them, and explain a term the first time if the reader may not know it.
- No metaphors, wordplay, or compressed phrases that need a second read.

**Acceptance criteria**
- Acceptance criteria are the conditions that must be true for the ticket to be complete and accepted.
- One observable result per bullet, written so anyone can check it.
- On a feature and a story each bullet is one numbered scenario in Given/When/Then form: `- AC1: Given <the starting state>, When <what the person does>, Then <what they see or get>.`
  Example: `- AC1: Given I am a logged-in user, When I click "Download Report", Then a report downloads in PDF format.`
  AC1 is the main scenario, the next ones are the other scenarios, and the edge cases come last.
  One Given, one When and one Then per scenario; a second When is a second scenario.
  A feature's scenarios are the few that show the whole feature works; each story's scenarios cover its own slice.
  Refer to a scenario by its number (`AC2`), and never renumber the ones that stay when one is added or removed.
- A task keeps plain bullets, as below: its criteria are checked by its test.
- On a task, start with the condition when there is one: `When the score is unknown, the ring shows "--" and all 20 blocks are dimmed.`
- Keep each bullet to one or two short sentences.
  Long exact values (SVG paths, colour lists, full commands) go in the description or the test plan, and the bullet points to them.
- Describe behavior, not implementation steps; the steps belong in the description.

**Two parts: what people read, then the planning and decision log**
- An epic, feature or story is also read by people outside the build: product managers, designers and testers.
  Its top part is for them: the opening (on a feature or story, its user story sentence), the why and the what (`## Intent`, `## Goals`, `## Non-goals`, `## Anti-goals`), and `## Acceptance criteria`.
- Write the top part so it makes sense to someone who has never seen the code.
  It holds no file names, code, commands, internal names or process words.
- Everything planning wrote sits below a rule, under `## Planning and decision log`.
  That is Constraints, Decided by the product owner, My calls, Left to the builder, Decisions so far, Not yet specified, Open questions, Notes, Plan, Tech doc and Evidence.
  The details a builder needs on a story (files, exact values, steps) go there too, under `## Notes`.
- The CLI keeps the two parts apart.
  Every body write of `compose linear create` and `compose linear update`, and every `compose decide`, puts each section on its side of the rule.
  With the first section of the log it adds the rule, the heading, and a short note that says the log is the planning the product owner and compose did, that it is for compose and its agents, and that nobody edits it by hand.
- Never write the rule, the heading or the note yourself, and never move a section across the rule.
- Change the log only through compose's commands (`compose linear update --section`, `compose decide`), never by hand in Linear.
- A task is its implementer's brief: it has one part and no log.

**Before and after**
- Title before: `Draw the Job card's No score in muted foreground without the plain info icon`.
  After: `Job card: show "No score" in muted text, without the info icon`.
- Title before: `Make text-lg 18/26`.
  After: `Typography: set text-lg to 18px with a 26px line height`.
- Criterion before: `Unknown and pending draw all 20 blocks data-lit="false" with fill-score-segment-{i} and opacity-25, and "--".`
  After: `When the score is unknown or pending, all 20 blocks are dimmed and the ring shows "--".`
  The exact attributes go in the test plan.

Before you save a ticket, read it once as that reader.
Rewrite any sentence you had to read twice.
