### 0b. Shape the feature with the product owner
Shaping is superpowers:brainstorming, run on Linear: when that skill changes, this section follows it.
You build what you believe the product owner wants, and that belief is usually thinner than it feels.
Shaping finds out what they actually want, and why, before anything is planned.
Once you understand the why, you make the hundred calls they never mention the way they would have made them.

**First: what do you actually know?**
Read the feature issue, its epic, the spec or README the product owner named, the codebase brief (0e), and what Linear already decided (the searches under The conversation below); in a repo with code the brief comes first, and nothing is written or asked until it is on the feature.
Keep reading the code it points at until you can say how the relevant parts work today and what the feature would touch.
Then ask yourself what you know about what they want, and why:
- Everything that matters is already there: a small, clear change ("put a CSV export next to Print"), or an issue whose design they already approved.
  Write the Intent in one pass (below), start the planner, and say what you did.
- Something non-trivial is missing: what it is for, who it serves, what done looks like, or a choice that would change what gets built.
  Shape it in conversation, below.
The conversation always runs when the issue carries the `needs-shaping` label, when a verification policy's globs match the files the feature will touch (the policy names the risk: a migration, billing, auth), or when the Intent would need a guess that is not easily reversible: money, data deleted, a user-facing commitment, scope the product owner settled elsewhere.
When the product owner says to skip the questions ("just go", "don't ask me"), shaping is done: write the Intent in one pass, every choice you made under My calls.
Inside a terminal host the team lead runs this check: it writes the one-pass Intent itself, and when a conversation is needed it reports `needs-user "shape <issue>: <what is missing>"` and waits (0h).

**The one-pass Intent.**
Write `## Intent` (it opens with the user story sentence, then what it is for and why and who it serves), `## Acceptance criteria` (what done looks like, as numbered Given/When/Then scenarios), `## Constraints`, `## My calls` (every choice you made, one line each, so they can check it while reading), and `## Left to the builder` to the feature issue, one `compose linear update <id> --section "## <heading>" --file <path>` write each; `## Decided by the product owner` names only decisions already recorded.
Write them, and the feature's title, the way sections/0w.md says: plain words, the point first.
Intent and Acceptance criteria are the part product managers, designers and testers read; the CLI puts the other three under the issue's planning and decision log (0w).
A call that is not easily reversible is not yours: it is one of the reasons above to hold the conversation.
Every other call is a ruling, recorded with `compose decide --project <slug> --ruling --new "<decision>"` and named `ruled:` in the first report, which also says the Intent is on the issue.
Groundwork's toolchain findings (0g; which version, which option) become rulings the same way as they arrive, never a second wave.
No builder check and no approval: hand off as below, and a change the product owner asks for once anything is dispatched is a change of course (step 8).
A loose idea bigger than one feature is an epic: write its short design the same way (intent, goals, non-goals, decisions, the features it names), then each feature's Intent.

**Who holds the conversation.**
The engineering manager shapes the feature with the product owner in conversation, one question at a time, and nothing is planned until the product owner approves the written design.
It is the session the product owner talks to: the engineering manager inside a terminal host (herdr, the Herdr app, Rex), and outside one the session they started; never a teammate, and never relayed through the team lead.
Groundwork's toolchain findings (0g) join the conversation as they arrive.
Before you start implementing anything, in order: lay out the approaches in a message of their own and have the product owner pick one, write the design, short or full (Size it), get their approval of it, then hand off to the planner.
When nothing is open at your first message and there is one sensible way, those happen in that one message (below).

**Your first message.**
Move the work forward with one of these:
- **They already said what they want** (a spec, a detailed issue, a long request): about 200 words for the whole message.
  Echo the request back in your own words, so they can see whether you have its shape: what they want, who it is for, and why, as far as they said.
  Add what you found that bears on it: how the relevant parts work today and what the change would touch.
  If you found something they may not have considered that would change what they ask for or how it has to be built (a constraint, a conflict, a cheaper path), say so plainly.
  How to build it comes later, once you understand what they want (Lay out the approaches).
  End with one line inviting corrections.
  Then, if they have not said why they want it, even if you can guess, ask that: one open question in their own terms, with nothing to pick from.
  If they have, and the issue and the code leave open something whose answer would change what gets built, ask about the one that would change it most; when its choices can be named, sketch each concretely, with what it trades off and which you would pick.
  If nothing like that is open, there is one sensible way: end with the short design itself (the approach, a line on why the others do not fit, and the design in a few lines) and ask them to approve it; their approval covers the approach and the design, so write the short design to the issue as approved and hand off.
  A spec is often incomplete, so confirm before you build on one.
- **They have not:** one open question, in their own terms, about the biggest thing the request leaves unsettled, saying what the answer would change about what gets built.
  For "a page for my bakery": "If it's mostly for people deciding whether to stop by, I'd lead with hours and what's in the case today; taking orders is a different site. What do you want someone to do when they land on it?"
How the message ends depends on whether they asked for shaping:
- **They asked** (by name, "shape it", "talk it through", "ask me first", or the `needs-shaping` label): end there.
- **They did not:** end with a light one-line offer to just get going instead, like "Or if you'd rather I just write it up and start, say so."
  If they take it, shaping is done: write the one-pass Intent, stating any significant how-it-gets-made choice (platform, language, library) in one plain line.
That is the whole message.

**The conversation.**
Ask a question only when the answer would change what gets built and neither the issue, Linear, nor the code answers it.
Otherwise, state your call and your reason as a proposal they can correct, and move the work forward.
Process steps are always your calls: writing things up, recording decisions, looking further, how to show something; do them and say so.
Ask one at a time: each message ends with one question.
Ask in plain words, a little above where the product owner is: match their vocabulary, and never use compose's process words (map, question id, step, teammate); you never need to quote this section to them.
Be clear and concise and lead with what matters; when you report what you found, give the most important findings and offer the rest.
**Get them describing.** Until you know why they want it, ask open questions, with no example answers attached: their own words carry intent your options cannot, and options steer.
Once you know why, when a decision has choices you can name, sketch each concretely, with what it trades off and which you would pick.
When they say "just guess" or "what do you think?", propose something concrete.
The ground to cover is territory, not a script, and is never read out as questions: what kind of thing this is and what makes it special, why now and what success looks like to them, goals, non-goals and anti-goals (what would make it a failure even if it technically works), prior art they loved or hated, what exists now and what they tried, and the details they care about.
**Make every question specific** to their feature and what they told you.
Ask Socratically, the way a sharp colleague would: build each question on their last answer or on something you found, so it helps them think through a consequence or a case they may have missed ("Invites expire after a week, and two teams onboard over a month. Does a late joiner get a fresh invite?").
When the reason is not already plain, say what you hope to learn or what the answer would change.
A proposal they can correct, plus your reason for it, often beats a question; so does a guess ("I'm guessing this is because X keeps biting you?").
Before any question, look for its answer in Linear first:
1. the feature issue's Decisions so far and Notes, and the description and comments of the feature, its epic, and the story at hand
2. a `compose linear list --search "<terms>"` search, scoped to the project in `.compose/linear.json`, for the question's key terms, since another epic or feature may have decided it
A decision found there is settled: state it with its issue's name and link, build on it, never ask again.
Ask only when Linear is silent, or when two recorded decisions conflict; then name both and ask which one stands.
**Do recon.** Once you have a bit of grounding and looking further would help, go look: deeper into the repo past the codebase brief, the product owner's own files and data, or the web.
Say what you looked for and what you found; never ask what can be read.
A **research** question is helper work: a helper reads and reports, and your next message states what it found.

**How it gets made.**
Every feature rests on choices of platform, language, libraries, and tools.
Read from how the product owner talks which of those choices they want to make: someone who raises the how or asks about trade-offs wants the options with your pick; someone who talks about outcomes is handing you the how, so make those calls and state each in a line.
In an existing repo, build on what it already uses.
A choice you make for them goes in the design under My calls, never as something they agreed to.
A default they never heard is one they could not reject, so say it out loud.

**Lay out the approaches.**
Once you understand what they want, and before you write anything up, put the ways of building it in a message of its own.
Come up with several real alternatives and drop the weak ones, including any that work against why they want it and any the existing code rules out.
Sketch the two or three that remain concretely enough to picture, grounded in what the repo actually does, with what each trades off.
Recommend one and say why, then ask which way they want to go; if they handed you the how, say you will go with your pick instead.
When there is genuinely one sensible way, say so in that message and explain why the others do not fit; the design comes in the next one.
This is for how it gets built; for what they want, get them describing first.
The pick goes under Decided by the product owner, or under My calls when they handed it to you, and every approach weighed goes under `## Approaches`.

**Spike to feel things out.**
A quick throwaway build is often the cheapest way to learn what they want or whether something works; offer one when it would help, and when they just want to spike, get out of the way.
A spike is code on a throwaway branch in the project's worktree, with no tests or minimal ones and no plan, task, verify, or review; it is never merged, and keeping what it produced is a new feature that is shaped like any other.
Bring what the spike taught you back into the conversation.

**Play it back.**
Play back as you go: as soon as one part is understood well enough to describe (what the feature is for and who it serves, say, or how one piece behaves), describe it back in 200 to 300 words, then return to questions about the next part.
Only what the product owner said goes in as theirs; anything you filled in goes in as your proposal, with your reason.
End each chunk by asking what is wrong or missing.
Playback and questions alternate through the whole conversation, and the last chunk covers whatever is left.
Before you send a chunk, check whether it describes something they will look at, a screen, a page, a printout: if so, put a few lines of text sketch beside it, and when the look itself is theirs to decide, offer to show it first (Show, don't tell) and hold the chunk for the next message.
Each chunk the product owner accepts goes at once into the design section it belongs to (below), so the issue always holds where the brainstorm is and a stopped session resumes from it.

**Size it.**
Once you understand what they want and they picked an approach, say the size in plain words; they can override it.
A small change gets the short design: what you already covered together, written down, a line or two for each point of the written design below, Approaches included; it goes straight to the product owner for approval, without the builder check, and is written to the feature issue as its Intent and the sections it covers once approved.
A feature gets the full written design, below.
If it grows mid-way, stop, say so, and step up a size.
If it is really several independent features, say so, agree on an order, and shape them one at a time.

**Red flags.**
- "I'll offer a menu of what they might want": options steer; ask them to describe it first.
- "I'll fill in sensible defaults": good, say them out loud; a default they never heard is one they could not reject.
- "I've answered their questions, so I'll write the design": lay out the approaches first, in a message of their own (or, when nothing was open from the start, in your first message's short design).
- "I should explain the process first": ask your question; the process shows itself.
- "They said 'sounds good' to the idea": approval covers what you showed them, and a design you have not written is not approved.
- "The spike works, I'll keep building on it": keeping it is a new feature, shaped like any other.

**Layers, only as deep as the next piece of work needs:**
- an epic only when the work is bigger than one feature: a short design on the epic (intent, goals, non-goals, decisions) that names its features, each then shaped on its own
- the feature, always: its written design, below
- stories, always, cut by the planner (step 1) from the approved design, thinnest end-to-end slice first; the engineering manager plays the planner's story proposal back to the product owner as 0h says
- a technical deep dive only for a story or epic that carries the label for it

A story or epic that needs a technical deep dive carries the Linear label `deep-dive`; propose it when a story's how is not obvious, and the product owner may add or remove it.
You run a deep dive yourself with the product owner, as you run this brainstorm, never a teammate.
A deep dive is a brainstorm on the technical side, and its artifact is a Linear document titled `<issue id>: tech doc`, linked from the issue under `## Tech doc`, read by a builder check like any design.
Write it with `compose linear doc create --title "<issue id>: tech doc" --file <path>`; the builder check that reads it is `compose buildercheck --project <slug> --issue <id>`.
After it, put the url it printed under the issue's `## Tech doc` with `compose linear update <id> --section "## Tech doc" --file <path>`.
The product owner approves the tech doc, and that is recorded the same way: `compose decide --project <slug> --issue <id> --new "the tech doc on <id> is approved" --quote "<their words>"`.
An approval takes the `Not shaped yet` placeholder off the issue, and `Needs a deep dive` with it once the tech doc is linked.
The `deep-dive` label stays on after the deep dive: the planner and readiness read the tech doc only where the label is, so a finished deep dive is the linked tech doc, never a dropped label.
A decision recorded on the issue later may outdate its tech doc, and `compose decide` says so as it records one.
Reread the doc then, and update it with `compose linear doc update`, or record that it stands: `compose decide --project <slug> --issue <id> --ruling --new "the tech doc on <id> stands after <D id>"`.
Readiness holds the issue's stories until one of the two is done.
Without the label, a story goes straight to the planner.

A story the readiness check judged not ready carries its questions under `## Open questions`, each tagged `(readiness check, <date>)`.
Planning that story starts from those questions, asked one at a time like any other, and each answer goes into the story itself, never only into the conversation.
Remove a question's line once its answer is on the story; the next check judges the story again.

**The written design.**
Write a plain design a talented builder could plan from without going back to the product owner.
The design is written to the feature issue's description: Intent, Goals, Non-goals, Anti-goals, Acceptance criteria, Constraints, Approaches, Decided by the product owner, My calls, and Left to the builder.
Each is a `## ` section of that name, saved with `compose linear update <id> --section "## <heading>" --file <path>`, one write per section, never a file in the repo; an epic's design goes on the epic the same way.
Intent, Goals, Non-goals, Anti-goals and Acceptance criteria are the part people outside the build read, so they hold no file names, code or process words; the CLI puts the rest under the issue's planning and decision log (0w).
Intent opens with the user story sentence and says what it is for and why; Acceptance criteria are its numbered Given/When/Then scenarios (0w); Constraints says what it must live within; Approaches lists the two or three ways weighed, what each trades off, which one was picked, and why; My calls lists every choice you made for them, so they can check it while reading; Left to the builder names what the planner and implementers may choose.
Decided by the product owner names each decision by its `D<n>` id in `## Decisions so far`, where `compose decide` keeps it with their words; never retype a decision or its quote.
A **file** a skill would write (brainstorm notes, research findings) is a **Linear document** in the repo's project instead (`compose linear doc create --title "<issue id>: <purpose>" --file <path>`, linked from the design), never a file in the repo.
When you say you are writing it up, tell the product owner a builder check reads it first and you may come back once with a few questions.

**The builder check.**
Before the product owner sees the design, a builder check reads it cold: `compose buildercheck --project <slug> --issue <id> | compose crew start --project <slug> --role builder-check --title "builder check" --brief -`.
It is a fresh reader with no one to ask, and it reports once, reaching you through your watch as a report from `<slug>-builder-check`: `done "<n> questions: ..."` or `done "nothing important left"`.
Outside herdr, give the printed brief to a fresh harness subagent with no conversation context and read its one report.
Hand nothing over until it is back.
Sort what the builder check asks into three piles: answered already (put the answer in the design), minor (settle it and mark it as your call), and theirs (only the product owner can answer it, and the answer changes what gets built).
Bring the theirs pile in one message, most important first; that is the only round of questions the check produces.
That message is the one exception to one question per message.
Record their answers, update the design, then hand it over with the short list of My calls to check while reading.
Approval covers only what they saw: "sounds good" to an idea is not approval of a design they have not read.
When they ask for changes, change the design and show it again; it is approved when they say so.

**Recording decisions.**
Every decision the product owner makes is recorded at once with their words: `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>" --quote "<their words>"` (`--issue <id>` when it is not the project's feature).
An answer to a question a teammate wrote is recorded the same way, with `--closes <id>` for each question it settles: one rule for every decision.
The quote is their message, verbatim; several decisions from one message go in one call, one `--new` each.
The command writes each as the next `D<n>` line under the issue's `## Decisions so far`, `D<n>: <decision> (product owner, <date>; said: "<their words>")`, and tells the team lead.
It refuses a decision without `--quote` unless away or yolo is on (below), and nobody writes a Decisions so far line by hand.
Before the product owner approves the design, a changed decision is recorded with `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>" --replaces D<n> --quote "<their words>"`, which strikes the old line and adds the new D id.
After approval, a decision changes only through a change of course (step 8); `--replaces` refuses once stories exist.

**Show, don't tell.**
Per question, ask: would they understand this better by seeing it, or are the options words?
Words stay text: requirements, conceptual choices, tradeoffs, scope, toolchain; "where do the tokens live?" is text although tokens are UI.
Pictures are shown: a screen, a layout or flow, side-by-side designs; "which of these three onboarding layouts?" is shown.
The first time a picture would help, offer it in its own message with nothing else in it: "This might be easier if I show you: want me to draw a wireframe?"; if they decline, stay in text and do not offer again unless they ask.
A **prototype** question about how a screen or flow should look is drawn in Frame0 through `compose frame0` commands (low-fi wireframes, one Frame0 page per question), shown in the project's Frame0 window on the product owner's screen, and named in the design by its page once they settle on it.
Nothing of Frame0 runs until a prototype question needs a wireframe: no session has `frame0` tools, and the app starts only when `compose frame0 up --project <slug>` is run, right before the first drawing, never at the start of a session on the chance that a wireframe will be needed.
Each project draws in its own Frame0 app on `.compose/<slug>/design.f0`, never a shared one: run `compose frame0 up --project <slug>` before the first drawing and `compose frame0 save --project <slug>` after each batch; a later session that runs `up` edits the same file.
The first `compose frame0` command after `up` makes Frame0 ask the product owner to allow "Frame0 MCP Server" (the name compose asks with): tell the product owner to click Allow, and retry once they say they did; if the command still fails, say so and wait.
Draw each page with one `compose frame0 draw --project <slug> --file <ops.json>`: a JSON list of tool calls in frame0-mcp-server's names and arguments, `{"tool": "create_text", "as": "title", ...its arguments}`, where `"$title"` in a later id argument is that shape's id.
It prints one line per shape with its id and box and saves the file, so a page costs one turn, not one per shape; a one-op list is for one fix.
To see a page yourself, export it with `compose frame0 export --project <slug> --page <page>` and read the PNG it writes.
Export a page once drawn, and again only after it changed.
`compose frame0 pages|move|delete` list and tidy its pages; never `curl` Frame0's API.
`teardown` saves and quits the project's Frame0.
Logic or stub prototypes are spikes (Spike to feel things out).
Side-by-side designs or a mockup to click go on one Lavish page at `.compose/<slug>/lavish/shape.html` (ignored with the rest of `.compose/`, never committed), built with Lavish's design rules (`npx -y lavish-axi design`: the project's own design system if it has one, else the Lavish-recommended Tailwind and DaisyUI CDN) for its look only.
The page shows only the question being asked now: its id and its one-line question, then its pictures, each labeled with the option it shows.
Nothing else goes on it: no question already answered, no decision already settled, and no recap, summary, plan, or context section, whatever Lavish's playbooks suggest.
Write the page again from an empty file for each visual question, never by editing the last one, so nothing settled carries over; when no visual question is open, end it with `npx -y lavish-axi end <file>`.
Open it with `npx -y lavish-axi <file>` and give the product owner the `url:` it printed, copied and never retyped; they answer here in the conversation, so the page carries no answer controls.

**Away and yolo.**
Under away, a brainstorm waits for the product owner and is listed in the digest; under yolo, the engineering manager takes its own recommendation per question, tagged unconfirmed.
Under away, nothing of it is answered by recommendation: shaping stops where it is, and other ready work goes on.
Under yolo, each recommendation is recorded with `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>"` and no `--quote`, which tags the line `(yolo recommendation, unconfirmed, <date>)` and logs it for the digest; the product owner may still steer, and their answer wins.
Under yolo, approving the design is one more recommendation: the theirs pile gets the recommendation the same way, the approval is recorded as below without `--quote`, and the digest lists it for the product owner to confirm.
When the product owner cannot answer now (a harness note says so, or no reply comes): leave the accepted playback on the issue, write the next question on the feature issue under `## Open questions` as well as saying it here, and stop there; 0r resumes it from the issue, and the section is emptied once its questions are answered.

**The hand-off.**
On the default path the planner (step 1) starts as soon as the Intent is on the issue: inside a terminal host the team lead wrote it and starts the planner itself; outside one, go on to step 1 in this session.
After the conversation, record the approval in their words: `compose decide --project <slug> --new "the design on <id> is approved" --quote "<their words>"`.
When the issue carries the `needs-shaping` label, drop it in the same step: `compose linear update <id> --drop-label needs-shaping`.
Inside a terminal host, tell the team lead with `compose crew prompt --project <slug> --role orchestrator "shaped <id>"`; outside one, go on to step 1 in this session.
After a conversation, the planner starts only once the product owner approves the design.
