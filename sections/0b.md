### 0b. Shape first, always
The engineering manager shapes the feature with the product owner in conversation, one question at a time, and nothing is planned until the product owner approves the written design.
It is the session the product owner talks to: the engineering manager inside herdr, and outside herdr the session they started; never a teammate, and never relayed through the team lead.
It runs on every project, never skipped, even when a README, ticket, or spec seems to settle everything: a spec still leaves choices open (data model, scoring, toolchain, UX), and a choice made silently is one the product owner never saw.
In a repo with code, the codebase brief comes first (0e): no question is asked until the brief is on the feature, and no question asks what the code already answers.
Groundwork's toolchain findings (0g; which version, which option) join the conversation as they arrive.
Before the first message, read the feature issue, its epic, the spec or README the product owner named, and the codebase brief.

**The first message** is two lines and nothing else.
First, one line saying that if they would rather skip the questions, they can say so.
If they do, write the design from what you have, with every choice you made under My calls, and go on to the builder check; approval still waits for them.
Then one open question that gets them describing a real moment or a concrete picture, such as "What is the moment you find yourself wishing this existed?" or "Who will use this, and what are they trying to get done?".

**The conversation.**
Each message ends with one question: a single sentence, with no list of example answers after it.
Ask in plain words, a little above where the product owner is: match their vocabulary, and never use compose's process words (map, question id, step, teammate).
Open questions come first; a short menu of options only when the product owner is stuck, and a concrete proposal when they say "just guess" or "what do you think?".
A menu for a stuck product owner is itself the one question: its few options are named inside that sentence, never listed after it.
Their own words carry intent your options cannot: options steer, so ask them to describe it first.
Each question follows from the last answer.
The ground to cover is territory, not a script, and is never read out as questions: what kind of thing this is and what makes it special, why now and what success looks like to them, goals, non-goals and anti-goals (what would make it a failure even if it technically works), prior art they loved or hated, what exists now and what they tried, and the details they care about.
Questions that work anchor in specifics: "Walk me through the last time...", "If it could only do one of these well, which?", "What would make you wince if I got it wrong?".
A guess they can correct ("I'm guessing this is because X keeps biting you?") often beats a question.
Before any question, look for its answer in Linear first:
1. the feature issue's Decisions so far and Notes, and the description and comments of the feature, its epic, and the story at hand
2. a `compose linear list --search "<terms>"` search, scoped to the project in `.compose/linear.json`, for the question's key terms, since another epic or feature may have decided it
A decision found there is settled: state it with its issue's name and link, build on it, never ask again.
Ask only when Linear is silent, or when two recorded decisions conflict; then name both and ask which one stands.
Never ask what can be read: when the repo past the codebase brief, a document, or the web would answer it, offer to go look, say what you would look for, and go only when they say yes.
A **research** question is helper work: a helper reads and reports, and your next message states what it found.
Think wide privately: before proposing anything, come up with several approaches and drop the weak ones, including any that fight what they said about why.

**How it gets made.**
Find out early which choices of platform, language, libraries, and tools the product owner wants to make, which they hand to you, and what they already have to work with.
Someone with strong views will want to talk it through; someone counting on you needs sensible defaults, said in plain words.
In an existing repo, build on what it already uses.
A choice you make for them goes in the design under My calls, never as something they agreed to.
A default they never heard is one they could not reject, so say it out loud.
Before writing the design, bring the two or three approaches that survived, each with what it changes and costs, and the one you recommend with why, as one question; the choice goes under Decided by the product owner, or under My calls when they hand it to you, and a change small enough to approve in chat skips this.

**Play it back.**
As soon as one part is understood, describe it back in 200 to 300 words, mark every guess as a guess, and ask what is wrong or missing.
A part is, for example, what the feature is for and who it serves, or how one piece behaves.
Only what the product owner said goes in as theirs.
Playback and questions alternate through the whole conversation, and the last chunk covers whatever is left.
Each chunk the product owner accepts goes at once into the design section it belongs to (below), so the issue always holds where the brainstorm is and a stopped session resumes from it.

**Size it.**
Once you understand what they want, say the size in plain words; they can override it.
A change small enough to describe in chat is approved in chat: write the approved description to the feature issue as its Intent, then hand off as below.
Anything bigger gets a written design.
If it grows mid-way, stop, say so, and step up a size.
If it is really several independent features, say so, agree on an order, and shape them one at a time.

**Layers, only as deep as the next piece of work needs:**
- an epic only when the work is bigger than one feature: a short design on the epic (intent, goals, non-goals, decisions) that names its features, each then shaped on its own
- the feature, always: its written design, below
- stories, always, cut by the planner (step 1) from the approved design, thinnest end-to-end slice first; the engineering manager plays the planner's story proposal back to the product owner as 0h says
- a technical deep dive only for a story or epic that carries the label for it

A story or epic that needs a technical deep dive carries the Linear label `deep-dive`; propose it when a story's how is not obvious, and the product owner may add or remove it.
You run a deep dive yourself with the product owner, as you run this brainstorm, never a teammate.
A deep dive is a brainstorm on the technical side, and its artifact is a Linear document titled `<issue id>: tech doc`, linked from the issue under `## Tech doc`, read by a builder check like any design.
Write it with `compose linear doc create --title "<issue id>: tech doc" --file <path>`; the builder check that reads it is `compose buildercheck --project <slug> --issue <id>`.
After it, put the url it printed under the issue's `## Tech doc` with `compose linear update <id> --section "## Tech doc" --file <path>`.
The product owner approves the tech doc, and that is recorded the same way: `compose decide --project <slug> --issue <id> --new "the tech doc on <id> is approved" --quote "<their words>"`.
Without the label, a story goes straight to the planner.

A story the readiness check judged not ready carries its questions under `## Open questions`, each tagged `(readiness check, <date>)`.
Planning that story starts from those questions, asked one at a time like any other, and each answer goes into the story itself, never only into the conversation.
Remove a question's line once its answer is on the story; the next check judges the story again.

**The written design.**
Write a plain design a talented builder could plan from without going back to the product owner.
The design is written to the feature issue's description: Intent, Goals, Non-goals, Anti-goals, Constraints, Decided by the product owner, My calls, and Left to the builder.
Each is a `## ` section of that name, saved with `compose linear update <id> --section "## <heading>" --file <path>`, one write per section, never a file in the repo; an epic's design goes on the epic the same way.
Intent says what it is for and why; Constraints says what it must live within; My calls lists every choice you made for them, so they can check it while reading; Left to the builder names what the planner and implementers may choose.
Decided by the product owner names each decision by its `D<n>` id in `## Decisions so far`, where `compose decide` keeps it with their words; never retype a decision or its quote.
A **file** a skill would write (brainstorm notes, research findings) is a **Linear document** in the repo's project instead (`compose linear doc create --title "<issue id>: <purpose>" --file <path>`, linked from the design), never a file in the repo.
When you say you are writing it up, tell the product owner a builder check reads it first and you may come back once with a few questions.

**The builder check.**
Before the product owner sees the design, a builder check reads it cold: `compose buildercheck --project <slug> --issue <id> | compose crew start --project <slug> --role builder-check --title "builder check" --brief -`.
It is a fresh reader with no one to ask, and it reports once, reaching you through your watch as a report from `<slug>-builder-check`: `done "<n> questions: ..."` or `done "nothing important left"`.
Outside herdr, give the printed brief to a fresh harness subagent with no conversation context and read its one report.
Hand nothing over until it is back.
Sort what the builder check asks into three piles: answered already (put the answer in the design), minor (settle it and mark it as your call), and theirs (only the product owner can answer it, and the answer changes what gets built).
Bring the theirs pile in one message, most important first; that is the only round of questions the check produces.
That message is the one exception to one question per message.
Record their answers, update the design, then hand it over with the short list of My calls to check while reading.
Approval covers only what they saw: "sounds good" to an idea is not approval of a design they have not read.
When they ask for changes, change the design and show it again; it is approved when they say so.

**Recording decisions.**
Every decision the product owner makes is recorded at once with their words: `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>" --quote "<their words>"` (`--issue <id>` when it is not the project's feature).
An answer to a question a teammate wrote is recorded the same way, with `--closes <id>` for each question it settles: one rule for every decision.
The quote is their message, verbatim; several decisions from one message go in one call, one `--new` each.
The command writes each as the next `D<n>` line under the issue's `## Decisions so far`, `D<n>: <decision> (product owner, <date>; said: "<their words>")`, and tells the team lead.
It refuses a decision without `--quote` unless away or yolo is on (below), and nobody writes a Decisions so far line by hand.
Before the product owner approves the design, a changed decision is recorded with `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>" --replaces D<n> --quote "<their words>"`, which strikes the old line and adds the new D id.
After approval, a decision changes only through a change of course (step 8); `--replaces` refuses once stories exist.

**Show, don't tell.**
Per question, ask: would they understand this better by seeing it, or are the options words?
Words stay text: requirements, conceptual choices, tradeoffs, scope, toolchain; "where do the tokens live?" is text although tokens are UI.
Pictures are shown: a screen, a layout or flow, side-by-side designs; "which of these three onboarding layouts?" is shown.
The first time a picture would help, offer it in its own message with nothing else in it: "This might be easier if I show you: want me to draw a wireframe?"; if they decline, stay in text and do not offer again unless they ask.
A **prototype** question about how a screen or flow should look is drawn in Frame0 through `compose frame0` commands (low-fi wireframes, one Frame0 page per question), shown in the project's Frame0 window on the product owner's screen, and named in the design by its page once they settle on it.
Nothing of Frame0 runs until a prototype question needs a wireframe: no session has `frame0` tools, and the app starts only when `compose frame0 up --project <slug>` is run, right before the first drawing, never at the start of a session on the chance that a wireframe will be needed.
Each project draws in its own Frame0 app on `.compose/<slug>/design.f0`, never a shared one: run `compose frame0 up --project <slug>` before the first drawing and `compose frame0 save --project <slug>` after each batch; a later session that runs `up` edits the same file.
The first `compose frame0` command after `up` makes Frame0 ask the product owner to allow "Frame0 MCP Server" (the name compose asks with): tell the product owner to click Allow, and retry once they say they did; if the command still fails, say so and wait.
Draw each page with one `compose frame0 draw --project <slug> --file <ops.json>`: a JSON list of tool calls in frame0-mcp-server's names and arguments, `{"tool": "create_text", "as": "title", ...its arguments}`, where `"$title"` in a later id argument is that shape's id.
It prints one line per shape with its id and box and saves the file, so a page costs one turn, not one per shape; a one-op list is for one fix.
To see a page yourself, export it with `compose frame0 export --project <slug> --page <page>` and read the PNG it writes.
Export a page once drawn, and again only after it changed.
`compose frame0 pages|move|delete` list and tidy its pages; never `curl` Frame0's API.
`teardown` saves and quits the project's Frame0.
Logic or stub prototypes go in code on a throwaway branch.
Side-by-side designs or a mockup to click go on one Lavish page at `.compose/<slug>/lavish/shape.html` (ignored with the rest of `.compose/`, never committed), built with Lavish's design rules (`npx -y lavish-axi design`: the project's own design system if it has one, else the Lavish-recommended Tailwind and DaisyUI CDN).
Open it with `npx -y lavish-axi <file>` and give the product owner the `url:` it printed, copied and never retyped; they answer here in the conversation, so the page carries no answer controls.

**Away and yolo.**
Under away, a brainstorm waits for the product owner and is listed in the digest; under yolo, the engineering manager takes its own recommendation per question, tagged unconfirmed.
Under away, nothing of it is answered by recommendation: shaping stops where it is, and other ready work goes on.
Under yolo, each recommendation is recorded with `compose decide --project <slug> --new "<decision>"` and no `--quote`, which tags the line `(yolo recommendation, unconfirmed, <date>)` and logs it for the digest; the product owner may still steer, and their answer wins.
Under yolo, approving the design is one more recommendation: the theirs pile gets the recommendation the same way, the approval is recorded as below without `--quote`, and the digest lists it for the product owner to confirm.
When the product owner cannot answer now (a harness note says so, or no reply comes): leave the accepted playback on the issue, write the next question on the feature issue under `## Open questions` as well as saying it here, and stop there; 0r resumes it from the issue, and the section is emptied once its questions are answered.

**The hand-off.**
When the product owner approves, record it in their words: `compose decide --project <slug> --new "the design on <id> is approved" --quote "<their words>"`.
When the issue carries the `needs-shaping` label, drop it in the same step: `compose linear update <id> --drop-label needs-shaping`.
Inside herdr, tell the team lead with `herdr agent prompt <slug>-orchestrator "shaped <id>"`; outside herdr, go on to step 1 in this session.
The planner (step 1) starts only after the product owner approves the design.
